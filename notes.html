On branding
Nov 2026

Leaving room

The empty part of a page isn’t unfinished. It is where the eye rests and the work can breathe.

Korean painting and calligraphy have a word for the space left unpainted: yeobaek (여백). It is usually translated as “blank space”, but that misses the intention. The empty part is placed as carefully as the brushstroke.

When we start working with someone, the first urge is to add: a pattern, a tagline, a second colour, a mascot. Most small practices don’t need more. They need room for the one true thing to be seen.

Leaving room is a decision, not an absence.

So we ask what can be taken away. A wrapper that is only paper and one stamp. A label with a single line of type. A website with wide margins. What remains carries more weight because nothing competes with it.

Room is also left for the person. An identity with space in it can grow: they can write on it, stamp it, and add the next chapter without asking us first.

Pro bono is not a discount

Our fourth value, Artistry, is the one we test ourselves against most often.

Free work carries a quiet risk. Because no one is paying, it slips down the list, the first draft gets accepted, and the person becomes someone to be grateful rather than a partner.

We think it should be the opposite. The people we work with have usually waited a long time to be taken seriously. What we make with them should be the best we can do, not what we can spare.

What that means in practice

We take on fewer projects so each gets real time. We draw several directions and discard most of them. We test ideas where they will live: the counter, the market table, the shop window. And we say no when we can’t do something properly yet.

Jeong is not pity. It is care between equals, given without keeping score. That is the standard we hold ourselves to.

Six questions for a first conversation

Before we bring any ideas, we bring these.

A first conversation isn’t a briefing. It is closer to an interview in which both sides are curious. These are the questions we return to most.

  1. What do you make, and how did you start?
  2. Who already loves your work, and what do they say about it?
  3. What do people most often misunderstand about what you do?
  4. Which part of the work would you never change?
  5. What would you like to be able to do in a year that you can’t do now?
  6. Honestly, how much time do you have to keep a brand going?

The last question matters more than it seems. An identity that needs a designer every week is not a gift. We design for the hours our partners actually have, and we write down their answers in their words, not ours.

Looking closely at wrappers

The paper around everyday things is often the most honest branding in the room.

Look at what small shops wrap things in. A bakery bag folded twice at the top. A florist’s newspaper cone. A butcher’s paper tied with string. Nobody designed these as a brand, yet you can recognise the shop from across the street.

What makes them work is repetition and care. The same fold, by the same hands, thousands of times. The habit becomes the mark.

So before drawing a logo, we look at what a practice already repeats every day, and ask whether one small change could give it more meaning: a stamp, a colour of string, a line of handwriting.
